Why translate English to Tamil?

Tamil is one of the world's great classical languages, spoken by more than 75 million people in Tamil Nadu, Sri Lanka, Singapore, Malaysia and across a global diaspora. It is an official language in three countries and one of the scheduled languages of India — and for many families abroad, it is the language of grandparents, festivals and home.

People use this translator for everyday things: messaging relatives in Madurai, helping children with Tamil homework, preparing for a trip to Chennai or the temple towns, understanding a Tamil song or film dialogue, or doing business with Tamil-speaking customers. Tamil for Beginners: A Friendly Starting Guide

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Getting Started

Tamil is one of the oldest living languages in the world — a classical language with literature more than two thousand years old, spoken today by over 75 million people in Tamil Nadu, Sri Lanka, Singapore, Malaysia and a worldwide diaspora. It can look intimidating because of its script, but its grammar is famously regular, and its everyday spoken form is warm and welcoming to learners.

A language that sings

Tamil sounds flow because most words end in vowels, and the language loves rhythm — Tamil poetry and film songs are a point of deep cultural pride. You will hear the famous retroflex sounds that English lacks, like the deep ழ (zha) in தமிழ் (thamizh) itself. Do not worry about perfecting them on day one; locals understand learners generously and light up when you try.

How Tamil sentences are built

English puts the verb in the middle: "I eat rice." Tamil puts the verb at the end: நான் சோறு சாப்பிடுகிறேன் (naan soru saappidugiren — literally "I rice eat"). Once your ear accepts subject–object–verb order, Tamil sentences become predictable. There are no articles like "a" or "the", and plurals and verb endings follow patterns rather than long lists of exceptions.

The first words to learn

Start with survival words: வணக்கம் (vanakkam — hello), நன்றி (nandri — thank you), தண்ணீர் (thanneer — water), உணவு (unavu — food), எவ்வளவு? (evvalavu? — how much?) and எங்கே? (enge? — where?). Add the numbers one to ten and the question words, and you can already handle taxis, tea stalls and market shopping. Our everyday vocabulary guide gives you sixty of the most useful words in one place.

Learning the script

The Tamil script has 247 traditional letters, which sounds alarming until you see the system: 12 vowels, 18 consonants, and their combinations, which are written in a tidy grid. Most learners can read shop signs within a few weeks of short daily practice. Our alphabet guide walks through the whole system calmly.

A realistic study routine

Fifteen minutes a day beats a monthly marathon. Spend five minutes on script, five on new words, and five speaking aloud — even to yourself. Use the translator on this site to check sentences about your own life: your family, your job, your town. Personal sentences stick in memory far better than textbook ones.

Above all, speak from the first week. A single வணக்கம் at a Chennai tea stall will teach you more about Tamil hospitality than a chapter of grammar. Nandri — and welcome to the language.

The Tamil Alphabet: Understanding the Script

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · The Script

Tamil writing is one of the most systematic scripts in the world. What looks like a wall of beautiful curves is really a small kit of parts assembled by strict rules. Tamil tradition counts 247 letters, but you only need to learn 30 basic signs and one combining rule to read almost anything.

The vowels (உயிர் எழுத்துகள்)

The twelve vowels are called உயிர் (uyir — "life"), because they give the consonants their voice. They are: அ a, ஆ aa, இ i, ஈ ee, உ u, ஊ oo, எ e, ஏ ae, ஐ ai, ஒ o, ஓ oh and ஔ au. Short and long pairs matter: கல் (kal — stone) and கால் (kaal — leg) differ only in vowel length.

The consonants (மெய் எழுத்துகள்)

The eighteen consonants are called மெய் (mei — "body"): க் k, ங் ng, ச் ch, ஞ் nj, ட் t (hard), ண் n (deep), த் th, ந் n, ப் p, ம் m, ய் y, ர் r, ல் l, வ் v, ழ் zh, ள் l (deep), ற் r (strong) and ன் n. Written with a dot above, a consonant is "pure" — it carries no vowel. Remove the dot and the vowel "a" is understood.

Combination letters (உயிர்மெய்)

When a consonant meets a vowel, they fuse into one sign: க + இ = கி (ki). Eighteen consonants times twelve vowels gives 216 combination letters — the bulk of the famous 247. The good news: the vowel part changes in regular ways, so you learn a pattern, not 216 separate shapes. There is also one special letter, the ஃ (aytham), an old "h"-like sound that survives in a few words.

Grantha letters for borrowed words

Modern Tamil adds a few letters for sounds borrowed from Sanskrit and English: ஜ ja, ஷ sha, ஸ sa, ஹ ha and the joined க்ஷ ksha. You will meet them in words like ஸ்கூல் (school) on every street.

How to learn it

Learn the vowels first, then one row of consonants a day with its combinations. Read real things — bus boards, shop names, film posters — and check doubtful words in the translator. Within a month, Tamil script stops being decoration and starts being information.

How to Translate English to Tamil Accurately

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Better Results

Machine translation between English and Tamil has improved enormously, but the two languages are built very differently, so small habits on your side make a big difference to the result. These nine tips come from the errors we see most often.

1. Write complete sentences

Fragments confuse translators because Tamil verb endings carry information English leaves to context. "Going tomorrow" could be a question, a plan or a command. Write "I am going tomorrow" and the Tamil comes out clean: நான் நாளை போகிறேன் (naan naalai pogiren).

2. Keep sentences short

Tamil puts the verb last, so a forty-word English sentence becomes a long walk to the verb. Split long sentences into two or three short ones before translating; the output will be dramatically more natural.

3. Use punctuation properly

Commas and full stops tell the translator where ideas end. A missing full stop can glue two thoughts into one strange Tamil sentence. Question marks matter too — Tamil questions often end in the sound -ஆ (-aa), and the translator chooses it when it sees your "?".

4. Avoid slang and idioms

"It's raining cats and dogs" translated word by word produces very confused Tamil. Rewrite idioms plainly first — "it is raining heavily" — then translate. The meaning survives; the confusion does not.

5. Be careful with names and places

Translators sometimes translate names that happen to be ordinary words. If a person's or company's name must stay in English letters, translate the sentence in two parts and paste the name back in afterwards.

6. Choose the right level of respect

Tamil verbs and pronouns change with respect. English "you" becomes informal நீ (nee) or respectful நீங்கள் (neengal). For elders, officials and anyone you have just met, add words like "sir", "madam" or "please" to your English — translators usually respond by choosing the respectful Tamil forms.

7. Watch out for English words with several meanings

"Book" the noun and "book" the verb are different Tamil words. If the result looks odd, add a clarifying word in English — "book a ticket", "read a book" — and translate again.

8. Translate paragraph by paragraph

Long pasted documents drift. Work one paragraph at a time, and keep a note of names and technical terms so they stay consistent from start to finish.

9. Back-translate to check

Finally, paste the Tamil result back into the translator in the Tamil-to-English direction. If the English that returns matches what you meant, your translation is safe to send. Try it now on the translator — and keep our travel phrases handy if your translation is for a Tamil Nadu trip.

Essential Tamil Phrases for Travelling in Tamil Nadu

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Travel

Tamil Nadu rewards travellers who try even a little Tamil. English is widely understood in Chennai and the big towns, but a greeting in Tamil at a tea stall, a bus stand or a temple entrance changes the whole conversation. Here are the phrases that matter most, grouped by the moments you will actually use them.

Greetings and courtesy

Start every interaction with வணக்கம் (vanakkam — hello), palms together if you like. Ask எப்படி இருக்கீங்க? (eppadi irukkeenga? — how are you?) and answer நலமாக இருக்கிறேன் (nalamaaga irukkiren — I am fine). Thank everyone with நன்றி (nandri); it is short, sweet and always noticed.

Getting around

For auto-rickshaws and taxis: எவ்வளவு? (evvalavu? — how much?) settles the fare before you move. இங்கே நிறுத்துங்கள் (inge niruthungal — please stop here), இடது பக்கம் (idathu pakkam — left side) and வலது பக்கம் (valathu pakkam — right side) handle directions, and மெதுவாக போங்கள் (medhuvaaga pongal — please go slowly) is worth its weight in gold.

Food and drink

Tamil Nadu runs on filter coffee and banana-leaf meals. Ask ஒரு காபி (oru kaapi — one coffee), declare நான் சைவம் (naan saivam — I am vegetarian) if needed, and praise the food with மிகவும் சுவையாக இருக்கிறது (migavum suvaiyaaga irukkirathu — it is very tasty). For less heat: காரம் குறைவாக (kaaram kuraivaaga — less spicy).

Shopping and bargaining

இது எவ்வளவு? (idhu evvalavu? — how much is this?) opens every negotiation. If the price feels high, smile and ask கொஞ்சம் குறைக்க முடியுமா? (konjam kuraikka mudiyumaa? — can you reduce it a little?). Finish with நான் இதை எடுத்துக்கொள்கிறேன் (naan idhai eduthukkolgiren — I'll take this) when you are happy.

Emergencies and help

Memorise three lines just in case: உதவி! (udhavi! — help!), எனக்கு ஒரு மருத்துவர் வேண்டும் (enakku oru maruthuvar vendum — I need a doctor) and மருத்துவமனை எங்கே இருக்கிறது? (maruthuvamanai enge irukkirathu? — where is the hospital?). People in Tamil Nadu are quick to help a traveller in trouble.

Tamil Numbers 1 to 100: Counting Made Simple

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Numbers & Counting

Numbers are among the first things you need in any language. In Tamil Nadu you will use them for auto fares, market prices, hotel rooms, phone numbers and train times — often several times a day. The good news is that Tamil counting follows a clear pattern once you know the first ten.

The numbers 1 to 10

Everything starts here: ஒன்று (ondru — one), இரண்டு (irandu — two), மூன்று (moondru — three), நான்கு (naangu — four), ஐந்து (ainthu — five), ஆறு (aaru — six), ஏழு (ezhu — seven), எட்டு (ettu — eight), ஒன்பது (onbathu — nine) and பத்து (pathu — ten). Say them aloud a few times; vendors will recognise your effort instantly.

The tens: 20 to 100

From twenty the pattern becomes regular: இருபது (irupathu — twenty), முப்பது (muppathu — thirty), நாற்பது (naarpathu — forty), ஐம்பது (aimpathu — fifty), அறுபது (arupathu — sixty), எழுபது (ezhupathu — seventy), எண்பது (enpathu — eighty), தொண்ணூறு (thonnooru — ninety) and நூறு (nooru — one hundred). Numbers in between join ten and unit, so twenty-five is இருபத்தி ஐந்து (irupathi ainthu).

Hundreds, thousands and lakhs

For bigger numbers: ஆயிரம் (aayiram — one thousand) and லட்சம் (latcham — one lakh, that is, one hundred thousand). India counts in lakhs and crores rather than millions, so a price quoted as ஐம்பது லட்சம் (aimpathu latcham) means fifty lakh rupees — five million. Knowing this one word prevents expensive misunderstandings.

Numbers in daily life

A few ready-made lines cover most situations. Asking a price: எவ்வளவு? (evvalavu? — how much is it?). Zero is பூஜ்ஜியம் (poojjiyam), which you will need for phone numbers, given digit by digit. For quantities, put the number first: இரண்டு டீ (irandu tee — two teas) will be understood in any tea stall in the state.

Practise by counting real things this week — steps, stops, cups of coffee — and test any number sentence in the translator before you use it. Numbers plus the phrases in our shopping guide make market day much easier.

Everyday Tamil Vocabulary: 60 Words You Will Use Daily

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Vocabulary

A language is built one useful word at a time. These sixty words were chosen because they come up constantly in Tamil homes, streets and shops. Learn ten a day and within a week you will be recognising Tamil everywhere — on signboards, in conversations and in film songs.

Home and family

Days, Months and Telling the Time in Tamil

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Time & Dates

"When?" is one of the most practical questions in any language. Whether you are catching a train, fixing a meeting or planning around the monsoon, you need days, dates and clock times. Tamil keeps this simple: a small set of words covers almost every plan you will make.

The seven days

The week runs: ஞாயிறு (gnaayiru — Sunday), திங்கள் (thingal — Monday), செவ்வாய் (sevvaai — Tuesday), புதன் (budhan — Wednesday), வியாழன் (viyaazhan — Thursday), வெள்ளி (velli — Friday) and சனி (sani — Saturday). Most are named after the sun, moon and planets, just like the English words Sunday and Monday.

Today, tomorrow and yesterday

Three words handle most planning: இன்று (indru — today), நாளை (naalai — tomorrow) and நேற்று (netru — yesterday). "See you tomorrow" is நாளை பார்க்கலாம் (naalai paarkkalaam) — a phrase worth using daily.

Parts of the day

The day divides into காலை (kaalai — morning), மதியம் (madhiyam — midday, noon), மாலை (maalai — evening) and இரவு (iravu — night). Greetings follow the same pattern — காலை வணக்கம் (kaalai vanakkam) for good morning and இனிய இரவு (iniya iravu) for a sweet night.

Months of the year

For everyday dates, Tamil Nadu uses the familiar English month names. But Tamil also keeps its own solar calendar, and its months appear on festival posters and almanacs: சித்திரை (chithirai, mid-April to mid-May) opens the year with Tamil New Year, followed by வைகாசி (vaigaasi), ஆனி (aani), ஆடி (aadi), ஆவணி (aavani), புரட்டாசி (purattaasi), ஐப்பசி (aippasi), கார்த்திகை (kaarthigai), மார்கழி (maargazhi — the music season), தை (thai — Pongal falls here), மாசி (maasi) and பங்குனி (panguni).

Asking and telling the time

Ask என்ன நேரம்? (enna neram? — what time is it?) or மணி எவ்வளவு? (mani evvalavu?). Answers use மணி (mani — o'clock): பத்து மணி (pathu mani — ten o'clock). Half past is அரை (arai): பத்தரை (patharai) is half past ten. An hour is ஒரு மணி நேரம் (oru mani neram) and a minute is ஒரு நிமிடம் (oru nimidam).

Combine these with the numbers guide and you can read any timetable in the state. For longer sentences, draft them in the translator and check the word order before you travel.

Family Words in Tamil: Relatives and Relationships

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Family

Family sits at the centre of life in Tamil Nadu, and Tamil has a precise word for almost every relationship — including the age order that English ignores. Learning these words does more than build vocabulary: it explains why half the people in a Chennai street seem to be someone's "annan" (elder brother) or "akka" (elder sister), related or not.

Parents and grandparents

Father is அப்பா (appa) and mother is அம்மா (amma) — two of the most loved words in the language, and among a Tamil baby's first. Grandfather is தாத்தா (thaathaa) and grandmother is பாட்டி (paatti). The general word for family itself is குடும்பம் (kudumbam).

Brothers and sisters

Tamil always asks: older or younger? An elder brother is அண்ணன் (annan) and an elder sister is அக்கா (akka) — words also used respectfully for any slightly older man or woman. A younger brother is தம்பி (thambi) and a younger sister is தங்கை (thangai); a younger sibling of either kind is often affectionately addressed by these words even by neighbours and shopkeepers.

Uncles and aunts

Here Tamil is more detailed than English. Your father's elder brother is பெரியப்பா (periyappa — "big father") and his younger brother is சித்தப்பா (chithappa). Your mother's brother is மாமா (maamaa), a word that also means father-in-law in many families — context makes it clear. Aunts include அத்தை (athai — father's sister) and பெரியம்மா (periyamma — mother's elder sister). Do not worry about memorising every branch at once; Tamils are forgiving teachers.

Children and the next generation

A son is மகன் (magan) and a daughter is மகள் (magal); a child is குழந்தை (kuzhandhai) and children are குழந்தைகள் (kuzhandhaigal). A husband is கணவன் (kanavan) and a wife is மனைவி (manaivi).

Talking about your family

Two sentence frames cover most introductions. "This is my mother" is இவர் என் அம்மா (ivar en amma). "I have two sisters" is எனக்கு இரண்டு சகோதரிகள் உள்ளனர் (enakku irandu sagodharigal ullanar). Notice என் (en — my) and எனக்கு (enakku — to me, I have) doing the heavy lifting; both return in our self-introduction guide.

Family questions are friendly conversation in Tamil Nadu, not prying — asking about someone's குடும்பம் shows you care. Practise your answers in the translator, and you will be ready for the warmest small talk in India.

Tamil Greetings and Goodbyes for Every Occasion

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Speaking

Every conversation in Tamil Nadu begins and ends with warmth, and the words are easy to learn. Master this small set of greetings and goodbyes and you will never be stuck at the start or the end of a chat again — in a home, a shop, an office or on the phone.

The all-purpose vanakkam

வணக்கம் (vanakkam) works at any hour, for any age, in any setting — hello, good day and respectful greeting in one word. Join your palms lightly as you say it and you have the complete Tamil greeting. When someone greets you first, simply return the same word with a smile. Vanakkam is also what you say when entering a temple or an elder's home, which makes it the single most useful word in this guide.

Greetings by time of day

Tamil also has time-specific greetings: காலை வணக்கம் (kaalai vanakkam — good morning), மாலை வணக்கம் (maalai vanakkam — good evening) and இனிய இரவு (iniya iravu — good night, literally "sweet night"). In casual daily life, plain vanakkam is more common than all three.

Asking how someone is

The standard question is எப்படி இருக்கீங்க? (eppadi irukkeenga? — how are you?), with the shorter நலமா? (nalamaa? — are you well?) between people who know each other. The usual answer is நலமாக இருக்கிறேன் (nalamaaga irukkiren — I am fine). Among friends you will also hear என்ன விசேஷம்? (enna visesham? — what's new?).

On the phone

Phone calls open with ஹலோ (halo — hello) or vanakkam. To ask who is speaking: யார் பேசுவது? (yaar pesuvadhu?). To say "I will call back", use நான் மீண்டும் அழைக்கிறேன் (naan meendum azhaikkiren). Calls to elders and offices often end with a respectful vanakkam rather than a casual bye.

Welcome and congratulations

Welcome a guest with நல்வரவு (nalvaravu — welcome). Congratulate with வாழ்த்துகள் (vaazhthugal — congratulations, greetings), wish a birthday with பிறந்தநாள் வாழ்த்துகள் (pirandhanaal vaazhthugal), and thank someone warmly with மிக்க நன்றி (mikka nandri — thank you very much). Festival wishes get their own guide — see our festivals article.

Saying goodbye — Tamil style

The classic parting is போய் வருகிறேன் (poyi varugiren — literally "I go and come back"), answered by போய் வாருங்கள் (poyi vaarungal — go and come). Between friends, பிறகு பார்க்கலாம் (piragu paarkkalaam — see you later) is the natural goodbye. The idea underneath is the same as the greeting: nobody really leaves; they only go and return.

Try one greeting today — at a shop, with a neighbour, anywhere — and build the habit with the phrasebook. For meeting someone properly for the first time, continue to our self-introduction guide.

Shopping and Bargaining in Tamil: Market Phrases That Work

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Shopping

Tamil Nadu's markets — the flower strings of Madurai, the silk shops of Kanchipuram, the banana markets of every town — run on friendly talk. Prices in small shops are often a conversation, not a label. These phrases will help you shop with confidence and bargain with a smile, which is exactly how it is done locally.

Entering and looking around

Start with வணக்கம் (vanakkam). If you are just browsing, say சும்மா பார்க்கிறேன் (summa paarkkiren — I am just looking). To ask for something specific: காட்டுங்கள் (kaattungal — please show me) followed by the item, or இதில் வேறு நிறம் உள்ளதா? (idhil veru niram ulladhaa? — do you have this in another colour?). The word for shop is கடை (kadai) and for market, சந்தை (sandhai).

Asking the price

The one question you will use everywhere: இது எவ்வளவு? (idhu evvalavu? — how much is this?). For things sold by weight, ask ஒரு கிலோ எவ்வளவு? (oru kilo evvalavu? — how much per kilo?). Answers come fast, so learn the numbers first. If you did not catch the reply, மீண்டும் சொல்லுங்கள் (meendum sollungal — please say it again) buys you a slower repeat.

Sizes, colours and trying things

For clothes and footwear: பெரிய அளவு உள்ளதா? (periya alavu ulladhaa? — do you have a bigger size?) and சிறிய அளவு (siriya alavu — smaller size). Common colours: சிவப்பு (sivappu — red), நீலம் (neelam — blue), பச்சை (pachai — green), கருப்பு (karuppu — black), வெள்ளை (vellai — white) and மஞ்சள் (manjal — yellow).

Bargaining, politely

In markets (less so in fixed-price shops), gentle bargaining is expected. Open with விலை கொஞ்சம் அதிகம் (vilai konjam adhigam — the price is a little high), then ask குறைக்க முடியுமா? (kuraikka mudiyumaa? — can you reduce it?). Accept with சரி, நான் இதை எடுத்துக்கொள்கிறேன் (sari, naan idhai eduthukkolgiren — okay, I will take this), or decline kindly with வேண்டாம், நன்றி (vendaam, nandri — no need, thank you).

Paying and finishing

Ask for the bill with பில் கொடுங்கள் (bill kodungal). Cash is பணம் (panam); the change you receive back is மீதி (meedhi). Most shops now accept UPI payments — யுபிஐ உள்ளதா? (UPI ulladhaa? — do you take UPI?) is a perfectly normal question in 2026.

Keep this guide open on market day alongside the phrasebook, and run any tricky sentence through the translator before you set out. Bargaining in Tamil, even imperfect Tamil, changes the whole shopping experience.

Emergency and Health Phrases in Tamil

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Emergency

Nobody plans an emergency, but a few memorised lines can make a frightening moment manageable. Tamil Nadu has good hospitals and helpful people everywhere; these phrases make sure you can ask for the right help quickly, for yourself or for someone else. Save this page offline before you travel.

Calling for help

The word that cuts through noise is உதவி! (udhavi! — help!). Add ஆபத்து! (aabathu! — danger!) if there has been a crash or a fall. To stop someone urgently: நிறுத்துங்கள்! (niruthungal! — stop!). India's single emergency number is 112, and an ambulance can also be reached on 108 — numbers worth saving in your phone alongside these words.

Getting medical help

The essentials: மருத்துவமனை எங்கே இருக்கிறது? (maruthuvamanai enge irukkirathu? — where is the hospital?), மருத்துவரை அழையுங்கள் (maruthuvarai azhaiyungal — please call a doctor), and ஆம்புலன்ஸ் அழையுங்கள் (ambulance azhaiyungal — call an ambulance). The word for a doctor is மருத்துவர் (maruthuvar) and for a hospital, மருத்துவமனை (maruthuvamanai).

Describing what is wrong

Start with எனக்கு உடல்நலம் சரியில்லை (enakku udalnallam sariyillai — I am not well). The word வலி (vali — pain) combines with body parts: தலைவலி (thalaivali — headache), வயிற்று வலி (vayitru vali — stomach ache), பல் வலி (pal vali — toothache). Other common words: காய்ச்சல் (kaaichal — fever), இருமல் (irumal — cough), இரத்தம் (iratham — blood) and ஒவ்வாமை (ovvaamai — allergy). "It hurts here" is இங்கே வலிக்கிறது (inge valikkirathu) — point as you say it.

At the pharmacy

Medicine is மருந்து (marundhu), and a pharmacy is usually signed "Medical" or மருந்தகம் (marundhagam). Useful lines: இந்த மருந்து உள்ளதா? (indha marundhu ulladhaa? — do you have this medicine?) and ஒரு நாளைக்கு எத்தனை முறை சாப்பிட வேண்டும்? (oru naalaikku ethanai murai saappida vendum? — how many times a day should I take it?).

Police and personal safety

For theft or trouble: காவல்துறையை அழையுங்கள் (kaavalthuraiyai azhaiyungal — call the police). "I have lost my bag" is என் பை தொலைந்துவிட்டது (en pai tholaindhuvittadhu), and "I don't understand" — எனக்கு புரியவில்லை (enakku puriyavillai) — is the honest phrase that slows any difficult conversation down to a manageable speed.

Tamil Question Words and Pronouns: Ask Anything

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Speaking

Questions are where a language starts working for you. With a handful of question words and pronouns you can ask for prices, directions, names and help anywhere in Tamil Nadu — and understand most of the answers. This guide gives you the complete set, with examples you can use today.

The question words

  • என்ன? (enna?) — What? இது என்ன? (idhu enna? — what is this?)
  • யார்? (yaar?) — Who? அவர் யார்? (avar yaar? — who is that person?)
  • எங்கே? (enge?) — Where? நிலையம் எங்கே? (nilayam enge? — where is the station?)
  • எப்போது? (eppodhu?) — When? பஸ் எப்போது வரும்? (bus eppodhu varum? — when will the bus come?)
  • ஏன்? (en?) — Why? ஏன் தாமதம்? (en thaamadham? — why the delay?)
  • எப்படி? (eppadi?) — How? எப்படி போவது? (eppadi povadhu? — how do I go?)
  • எது? (edhu?) — Which? எது நல்லது? (edhu nalladhu? — which is good?)
  • எவ்வளவு? (evvalavu?) — How much? இது எவ்வளவு? (idhu evvalavu? — how much is this?)
  • எத்தனை? (ethanai?) — How many? எத்தனை பேர்? (ethanai per? — how many people?)

The -aa trick: turning statements into questions

Tamil often makes a yes–no question by adding the sound -ஆ (-aa) to the end of a word. நல்லது (nalladhu — good) becomes நல்லதா? (nalladhaa? — is it good?). You already know it from நலமா? (nalamaa? — are you well?). Once you hear the pattern, Tamil questions become easy to spot in conversation.

The pronouns

  • நான் (naan) — I
  • நீ (nee) — you (informal, for friends and children)
  • நீங்கள் (neengal) — you (respectful, and also plural)
  • அவன் (avan) — he · அவள் (aval) — she · அவர் (avar) — he/she (respectful)
  • அது (adhu) — it, that · இது (idhu) — this
  • நாங்கள் (naangal) — we (not including the listener) · நாம் (naam) — we (including the listener)
  • அவர்கள் (avargal) — they

The two "we" words confuse learners at first, but the idea is friendly: நாம் (naam) invites the listener into the group — "let's eat" thinking — while நாங்கள் (naangal) talks about your own group. When unsure, நாங்கள் is the safer everyday choice.

Putting it together

Notice how short Tamil questions can be: உங்கள் பெயர் என்ன? (ungal peyar enna? — what is your name?) is just "your name — what?". Tamil often drops the verb "is" entirely. Build your own questions by combining this guide with the numbers and vocabulary lists, and test them in the translator before you use them.

Pongal, Deepavali and Tamil Festivals: Words and Wishes

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Culture

Festivals in Tamil Nadu are harvest thanks, temple lamps, new clothes and sweets shared with the whole street. If you can greet a festival in Tamil, you will be pulled into celebrations wherever you are. Here are the biggest days of the year, what they mean, and exactly what to say.

Pongal — the harvest festival

Pongal, in mid-January in the month of தை (thai), thanks the sun god for the rice harvest. Families cook sweet பொங்கல் (pongal — rice with jaggery) in a new clay pot outdoors; when it boils over, everyone shouts "பொங்கலோ பொங்கல்!" (pongalo pongal!) for abundance. The greeting is பொங்கல் வாழ்த்துகள் (pongal vaazhthugal — happy Pongal). The days around it honour cattle (Mattu Pongal) and family visits (Kaanum Pongal).

Deepavali — the festival of lights

Deepavali (Diwali) is celebrated in Tamil Nadu with early-morning oil baths, new clothes, sweets and fireworks, welcoming light over darkness. Greet with தீபாவளி வாழ்த்துகள் (deepavali vaazhthugal — happy Deepavali). Expect to be offered sweets everywhere you go; accept at least a little, and say மிகவும் சுவையாக இருக்கிறது (migavum suvaiyaaga irukkirathu — it is very tasty).

Karthigai Deepam — rows of lamps

In the month of கார்த்திகை (kaarthigai), homes light rows of small oil lamps — அகல் விளக்கு (agal vilakku) — on doorsteps and balconies. In Thiruvannamalai a huge beacon is lit on the hill and the whole town glows. It is the quietest, most beautiful of the light festivals.

Thaipusam and temple festivals

தைப்பூசம் (thaipusam) honours Lord Murugan; devotees carry decorated காவடி (kaavadi) arches to his temples in long, singing processions. Every temple also has its own annual festival with chariot processions — the great temple car is called a தேர் (ther). If you visit, dress modestly and greet people with வணக்கம் (vanakkam).

Tamil New Year

Tamil New Year, தமிழ் புத்தாண்டு (thamizh puthandu), falls in mid-April at the start of சித்திரை (chithirai). Homes display a tray of fruits, flowers and a mirror for the first auspicious sight of the year, and families eat a dish mixing sweet, sour and bitter flavours — a reminder that the year will hold all tastes. Wish everyone இனிய தமிழ் புத்தாண்டு வாழ்த்துகள் (iniya thamizh puthandu vaazhthugal — happy Tamil New Year).

The one phrase for every festival

If you remember nothing else, வாழ்த்துகள் (vaazhthugal — greetings, congratulations) plus the festival's name works all year round. For the everyday courtesies around festival visits — arriving, thanking, leaving — keep our greetings guide open, and check any longer message in the translator before you send it.

How to Introduce Yourself in Tamil

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Speaking

A confident self-introduction is the highest-value minute of Tamil you can learn. It answers the questions every new acquaintance in Tamil Nadu will ask — your name, your place, your work — and it turns strangers into hosts. Build yours line by line below, then say the whole thing aloud a few times.

Start with your name

Open with வணக்கம் (vanakkam), then என் பெயர் ___ (en peyar ___ — my name is ___). If someone asks உங்கள் பெயர் என்ன? (ungal peyar enna? — what is your name?), the same sentence is your answer. Names are not translated; simply place yours in the gap.

Say where you are from

Use நான் ___ இருந்து வருகிறேன் (naan ___ irundhu varugiren — I come from ___): நான் லண்டனில் இருந்து வருகிறேன் (naan landanil irundhu varugiren — I come from London). For where you live now: நான் இப்போது சென்னையில் வசிக்கிறேன் (naan ippodhu chennaiyil vasikkiren — I now live in Chennai).

Talk about your work

The pattern is நான் ஒரு ___ (naan oru ___ — I am a ___): நான் ஒரு ஆசிரியர் (naan oru aasiriyar — I am a teacher), நான் ஒரு மருத்துவர் (naan oru maruthuvar — I am a doctor). A student says நான் ஒரு மாணவன் (naan oru maanavan) or, for a woman, மாணவி (maanavi). Not sure of your job's Tamil name? Check it in the translator — professions translate cleanly.

Mention your family

Family talk is welcome talk here. "I have one elder sister" is எனக்கு ஒரு அக்கா இருக்கிறார் (enakku oru akka irukkiraar). Married speakers can add எனக்கு திருமணம் ஆகிவிட்டது (enakku thirumanam aagivittadhu — I am married). The full family vocabulary lives in our family guide.

Add your languages — and a humble line

Two honest lines that always win smiles: நான் தமிழ் கற்றுக்கொண்டிருக்கிறேன் (naan thamizh katrukkondirukkiren — I am learning Tamil) and கொஞ்சம் கொஞ்சம் தமிழ் தெரியும் (konjam konjam thamizh theriyum — I know a little Tamil). People will slow down, help you, and often teach you a word on the spot.

Close politely

Finish with உங்களை சந்தித்ததில் மகிழ்ச்சி (ungalai sandhithathil magizhchi — nice to meet you) and நன்றி (nandri — thank you). That is a complete, respectful introduction in under a minute. Practise yours with the phrases in the phrasebook, and you are ready for any first meeting in Tamil Nadu.

Tamil for Food Lovers: Ordering Food in Tamil Nadu

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Food & Drink

Food is Tamil Nadu's love language, and even a little Tamil transforms eating here — waiters light up, portions grow, and the best off-menu items appear. This guide covers the words for the dishes you will meet and the phrases that get them made your way.

Breakfast: the great tiffin

Morning "tiffin" is sacred: இட்லி (idli — steamed rice cakes), தோசை (dosai — crisp rice crepe; masala dosai wraps spiced potato), பொங்கல் (pongal — peppery rice and dal), வடை (vadai — savoury doughnut) and பூரி (poori — puffed bread). Everything arrives with சாம்பார் (saambaar — lentil stew) and coconut சட்னி (chutney). Order with ஒரு செட் இட்லி (oru set idli — one set of idli).

The banana-leaf meals

A full lunch is simply called சாப்பாடு (saappaadu — meals): rice with sambar, ரசம் (rasam — peppery tamarind soup), பொரியல் (poriyal — stir-fried vegetables), கூட்டு (koottu — vegetable and dal), a crisp அப்பளம் (appalam — papad), and a finish of curd rice. Say சாப்பாடு ரெடியா? (saappaadu rediyaa? — are meals ready?) at any small hotel around noon and you will be fed royally. Eat with your right hand, and fold the leaf towards you afterwards to say it was good.

Filter coffee and tea

The day's rhythm is set by காபி (kaapi — filter coffee), frothed between tumbler and davara until it foams. Order ஒரு காபி, சர்க்கரை குறைவாக (oru kaapi, sarkarai kuraivaaga — one coffee, less sugar). Tea is டீ (tee), and a small glass at a roadside stall is one of Tamil Nadu's cheapest pleasures.

Snacks, sweets and biryani

Evening snacks include பஜ்ஜி (bajji — vegetable fritters) and சுண்டல் (sundal — spiced chickpeas, famous at Marina Beach). Sweets to know: மைசூர் பாக் (mysore pak — ghee fudge) and ஜாங்கிரி (jangiri). For a celebratory meal, பிரியாணி (biriyani) — Dindigul and Ambur styles are local legends.

Making it your way

The control phrases: காரம் குறைவாக (kaaram kuraivaaga — less spicy), உப்பு குறைவாக (uppu kuraivaaga — less salt), சைவம் (saivam — vegetarian) and அசைவம் (asaivam — non-vegetarian). Praise with சூப்பராக இருக்கிறது! (superaaga irukkirathu! — it is superb!) and settle up with பில் கொடுங்கள் (bill kodungal — please give the bill).

How Tamil Sentences Are Built: Word Order and Verb Endings

Translate English to Tamil · Learning Guides · Grammar

Tamil grammar sounds complicated from the outside, but its sentences are built from a few steady habits. Learn these habits and you can both decode translations from this site and start assembling your own sentences with confidence.

Habit one: the verb comes last

English order is subject–verb–object: "She reads a book." Tamil order is subject–object–verb: அவள் புத்தகம் படிக்கிறாள் (aval puthagam padikkiraal — literally "she book reads"). When you check a translation, read it backwards from the verb and the sentence suddenly makes sense. Time words like இன்று (indru — today) usually sit early in the sentence.

Habit two: the verb ending tells you who

Tamil verbs change their tails to show both tense and person. Take "speak", பேசு (pesu): நான் பேசுகிறேன் (naan pesugiren — I speak), அவன் பேசுகிறான் (avan pesugiraan — he speaks), அவள் பேசுகிறாள் (aval pesugiraal — she speaks), அவர்கள் பேசுகிறார்கள் (avargal pesugiraargal — they speak). The ending -கிற்- (-kir-) marks the present tense; past and future swap that middle piece while the person endings stay familiar.

Habit three: questions end in -aa

As the questions guide shows, a statement becomes a yes–no question by adding -ஆ (-aa): வருகிறார் (varugiraar — he is coming) → வருகிறாரா? (varugiraaraa? — is he coming?). Question words like எங்கே (enge — where) slot into the sentence where the answer would go, without flipping the word order around as English does.

Habit four: negatives are simple

Two forms cover most negatives. For "not" with verbs, Tamil often uses இல்லை (illai): எனக்கு தெரியவில்லை (enakku theriyavillai — I don't know). For "will not / does not (habitually)", verbs take -மாட்ட்- (-maatt-): நான் வரமாட்டேன் (naan varamaatten — I will not come). And there is no separate word for "is" in the present: இது என் வீடு (idhu en veedu) simply means "this (is) my house".

Habit five: endings glue the sentence together

Instead of little words like "to", "from" and "in", Tamil glues endings onto nouns: வீடு (veedu — house) becomes வீட்டில் (veettil — in the house), வீட்டுக்கு (veettukku — to the house), வீட்டிலிருந்து (veettilirundhu — from the house). Plurals usually add -கள் (-gal): புத்தகங்கள் (puthagangal — books).

Build your first sentences

Try the pattern yourself: pick a pronoun from the pronouns list, an object from the vocabulary guide, and a verb — then check your sentence in the translator. Example: நான் தமிழ் படிக்கிறேன் (naan thamizh padikkiren — I am studying Tamil). If the translator agrees with you, you are no longer memorising Tamil — you are building it.
